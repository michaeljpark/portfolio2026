At a glance

Privacy law protects your data. Nothing pays you for it.

People give AI their thinking every day. They can't see what it's worth, where it goes, or who profits from it. TokenTrust treats that as a measurement problem, and builds the measurement that was missing.

Problem

People have no idea what their data is worth, can't see where it goes, and have no say in who profits from it.

Insight

Law already covers consent and control. Nothing makes the value come back, and nothing was built to.

Outcome

A governance platform built on a Data Quality Index that prices contributed data, refunds it in tokens, and comes with a Canadian regulatory path.

Overview

A measurement problem, not another privacy complaint.

As lead, my first decision was what we would not spend twelve weeks on. Plenty of work already says AI companies take too much. I set us a harder question: if data has value, what would it take to actually pay someone for it?

Asking it that way turned a moral argument into a measurement problem, and everything after followed from that.

The team

Five people across industrial and graphic design, none of us governance specialists

Snapshot

Data is scored. Contributions are paid back in tokens. That value still counts when it moves to another platform.

Problem definition

You don't own tokens. You only give data.

Most AI companies treat tokens as an internal meter for usage. Consent and control are already written into law. Getting the value back isn't, and that one gap is where I put the project.

Service framework

Three components, one loop.

Paying people for data was never a technical problem. Nobody had agreed how to measure the thing being paid for. I kept the service to three parts, because with any more the team couldn't explain it in one pass.

Components

What each one does in the loop

The exchange rate

Data Quality Index

Accuracy, completeness, consistency and freshness. Privacy sits outside the score as a gate: when it trips, nothing goes out at all.

The refund

Reward Token

You contribute data and get tokens back that you can spend on the service. This is what closes the loop.

The counterweight

Data Credit

A trust rating for companies. I added it last, once I saw the system had no way to hold the enterprise side to its end of the deal.

Prototyping

Give the team something concrete to disagree about.

A governance framework is easy to nod along to and hard to argue with. I held the interface decisions myself. With five people and twelve weeks, a design settled by vote would have drifted. I set the flow structure and the rules for each component, and the graphic designers built layouts against them and led the visuals for the final pitch, including the film.

Conclusion

The barrier was in the definition, not the technology.

Once DQI gave data a measurable identity, a refund stopped being a promise and became something you could calculate. Compliance stopped being a cost and became the floor the whole system stands on.

What leading this taught me

Decide what counts as evidence first

Splitting research across the team only worked where I'd decided in advance what would count as evidence. The parts I had to redo were the parts where I hadn't.

Critique as checkpoints

Bringing the advisors in along the way, not as approval at the end, is what kept the scope from drifting.

Protect one person's time for synthesis

In a twelve-week project, nobody else has room to hold the whole argument.
