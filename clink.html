At a glance

A canvas can hold everything. Finding your way in it is the hard part.

Canvas workspaces put a team's whole project on one surface, and then the surface gets in the way: it is hard to start with, the conversation about the work is hard to follow, and once the project grows nobody can find where anything is. Clink is built around finding the way.

Problem

Existing canvas workspaces are not intuitive, make communication hard to track, and make it hard to find what is where.

Question

Reframed on the first prototype: how can people find their way, efficiently, inside a project that keeps growing?

Outcome

A full-stack web app in closed beta, run as CNPT's internal workflow tool and incubating as a product of its own.

Overview

It started as a small part of something else.

Clink & Afterwork is two products that grew out of one project. Afterwork is a social network where students and junior professionals find their way into work they drive together. Clink is the dashboard for organising those projects, and it was never planned as a product at all.

01

From client work to a product

How the dashboard became Clink

  1. Client work

    CNPT started out building things for other people, then set out to make a first service under its own name.

  2. A mentorship program

    That service brought students and junior professionals into shared work. Afterwork was conceived here.

  3. A dashboard inside it

    The program needed a sub-component to organise the projects its people would run together.

  4. Clink

    Read against the market, the dashboard was a tool on its own. It now stands beside Afterwork, not inside it.

Problem definition

Canvas workspaces hold the work, and lose the people in it.

I led the problem definition. Looking at the canvas workspaces teams already use, the same three problems kept coming up, and none of them was about storing things. They were about finding your way.

01

What I found

Three problems with canvas workspaces

Not intuitive

An empty infinite surface asks a team to invent its own structure before it can start on the work.

Communication is hard to track

What was said about a card, and what was decided, sits apart from the card, so the thread is hard to follow back.

Hard to find what is where

As a board fills, finding a file, a task or the latest change means scanning the whole surface.

02

Reframed on the first prototype

The question the work started from

How might people find their way, efficiently, inside a project that keeps growing?

I led the build of Clink's first prototype, and it became the base for the question. Testing the direction against a working board, rather than a mockup, showed that a bigger canvas was not the answer; the way through it was. The exploration started there.

Discovery

Why an internal tool was worth expanding.

Turning an internal tool into a product is a strategic call, not a design one. Two things made the case, and the strategy that followed was to expand it independently: keep it as our internal workflow tool, and grow it from there into something other teams can pick up.

01

Two reasons

What made the case

Market value

Read against the tools teams already use, a workspace shaped around how a small team actually works together still looked worth making.

Project management

A small team rarely has someone whose whole job is coordinating it, so the coordinating has to live in the workspace itself. The dashboard was already doing that inside the program.

02

What the shift changed

From our tool to anyone's

  • From sub-component to product

    The dashboard stopped being scoped by the mentorship program and needed a direction of its own.

  • From our habits to the interface

    Conventions we carried in our heads had to become things the interface states, because another team arrives without our context.

Product

Three answers to one question.

Clink is an infinite canvas where a block can be a document, a poll, an availability grid, a reference card or a conversation, all on one surface. Each of its three ideas answers one of the problems: blocks that join make the canvas easy to start, organizing keeps a growing board readable, and wayfinding keeps the talk and the way next to the work.

01

Blocks

Everything is a block, and blocks join up

Write a note, drop in a PDF, paste a link, add a table, a chart, a to-do list or a calendar. Hold one block against another and they become one, the way a team builds a page together.

  • Typed blocksText, documents, pictures, PDFs, slides, tables, charts, votes and calendars, each moved, resized and connected the same way.
  • Join into oneText with text, a picture with its caption: dropped together, two blocks become one.
  • Work at the same timeWho is here, where they point and who wrote which line, live.
  • Connect ideasLines between blocks show how one idea leads to the next.
02

Organizing

A growing project, kept tidy

Projects pile up fast: references, drafts, notes from every meeting. Every block lines up on the same grid, so the board still makes sense in week ten.

  • Sections and pagesGroup the work by topic or by week, and fold a section away when it's done.
  • SortBy time, by author, by dock, or by section, with sections that say alike placed together.
  • To-do listsStages, priorities and due dates, with progress on the card.
  • Calendar and availabilityEveryone paints their free hours; the time that suits all of them becomes the meeting.
03

Wayfinding

Always know where to go next

Conversation points straight at the work. Come back after a weekend and Clink shows what changed and who changed it, so nobody has to ask where that file is.

  • DocksOne press takes you to the part of the board that matters.
  • Comments in placePinned to a block, with replies and @mentions, so the discussion moves with the work.
  • Team chatLinks people, blocks and comments; a mentioned block is one tap away.
  • What's newWhat changed since your last visit, and by whom.
04

How a team uses it

The same four steps for two people or a studio

  1. 01

    Start from a template

    Task Management sets up a to-do list with progress and a calendar; Brainstorming adds idea sections and a vote.

  2. 02

    Bring the team in

    Teammates join from the board's link as editors; a tutor or a client can join to view and comment.

  3. 03

    Work on one canvas

    Drafts and references go up, tasks get split and dated, and the talk happens in comments and chat as you go.

  4. 04

    Review and present

    Feedback on each draft, a vote on the options, and slides presented straight from the board.

Build

Build it, put it in front of people, review, redesign.

I took the full stack: the first prototype, then a real service with a closed beta. Every round ran the same loop. What I built went to real users, their feedback came back through a structure we ran ourselves, and a teammate's review of it set up the next round of interaction design.

01

The loop

One round, again and again

  1. MeBuild

    The board engine, the real-time back end and the server, in one pair of hands.

  2. Beta usersPut it in front of people

    A closed beta with tester sign-in: 20+ active users on real boards.

  3. In the productHear back

    Feedback sent from any board lands on our own feedback board: 30+ pieces so far.

  4. A teammate, meReview and redesign

    A teammate reviews what came back; I design the interactions it calls for.

The next round starts at Build
02

The full stack

Prototype first, then a real service

The first prototype was a board list and a board canvas. Making it a service people could rely on meant a back end that holds a team's board, its files and who is on it at the moment, and a way to let testers in before anyone else.

  • Board engine

    The canvas, its blocks and joins, docks, sorting, and a phone view that reads the board as a feed.

  • Real time

    Boards and comments in Firestore, files in Storage, presence and pointers in the Realtime Database.

  • Server

    Cloud Functions for the API, the feedback board, app connections and Clink MCP.

  • Closed beta

    Tester sign-in with a username and password, caps for everyone together, and analytics to see what is used.

03

A feedback structure of our own

Try it: the product's dialog

Any board's help button opens Give feedback: a suggestion or a bug, a name, what happened and up to four screenshots. It lands as a card on a Clink board of our own, linked to the board it came from, with no email or account detail written in. Users' feedback is read on the same kind of board it is about.

04

What the rounds changed

Interaction design shipped in the closed beta

The board as a feed on phones

On a phone the canvas reads as a chronological feed that scrolls like a page; nothing has to be dragged.

Sections that find each other

Sort by section places sections that are about the same thing one under another, not only ones that share words.

Docks that follow the view

The dock bar folds to the dock nearest the view and opens under the pointer, instead of sending you to a corner.

Threads people can fix

Your own replies in comments and chat can be edited and deleted, so a thread stays readable after a slip.

Story

Explaining it in the product's own hand.

A board is easier to show than to describe, so Clink is explained in two films and a set of drawings, all made from the product's own pieces and drawn in one marker ink.

Conclusion

We did not set out to make this one.

Clink & Afterwork is a work in progress, incubating through 2026 and 2027. The lesson I would carry forward is about where products come from: this one came out of building something else carefully enough to notice what we kept needing, and the sub-component turned out to be the part with a future of its own.

01

The open question

What incubation is for

Conventions that are obvious to the people who made them have to become obvious to a team that was not in the room. Every internal tool meets that question on the way out, and the loop of building, hearing back and reviewing is how Clink answers it.
